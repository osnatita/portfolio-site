On the Road, Again

ממשיכה את דרכו

Osnat Ita Skoblinski · Ynet, 27 December 2009 · Chapter one of seven

“I am setting out on the same journey, with the same book in my backpack.” Osnat Ita Skoblinski follows Jack Kerouac's On the Road on a journey that begins in San Francisco, and we go with her.

The poet Osnat Ita Skoblinski set off with a little money in her pocket, a copy of the book and a great deal of hope, following Jack Kerouac's On the Road, to travel those same roads more than fifty years later. In a special series of articles she will report to Ynet on what happens to her.

“Everybody wants to get to San Francisco, and what for? For God's sake and on this earth, what for? For the fun, for the thrills, for something that burns in the night.” (Kerouac, from On the Road)

First stop — San Francisco

I was too excited to sleep. Michael was sitting in the lobby with a pile of empty beer cans in front of him. “I need a cigarette,” I announced. “So do I,” he replied. It was five in the morning. San Francisco has no kiosks that stay open all night. We went into the beatnik bar above the shop. One lone customer was sitting there.

“Could I have a cigarette, please?” I asked him. “No, you could not,” answered the putz. Michael and I rolled drunk down the hill, and at the bottom of it a newsstand had opened and sold us the tobacco we were after. We climbed back up, went into the bar, I put one of my own cigarettes down next to the putz, and Michael asked for a Guinness. The barman refused to serve alcohol to a swaying Michael, so I ordered one for myself and we went up to the second floor.

“You see that chair?” said Michael, pointing. “That's Jack Kerouac's chair.” I examined the table with due respect and said to him: “Let's sit at the one opposite. That will be our table.”

I did not find Kerouac or his generation in San Francisco, and I had no need to find them: looking for them was the journey. In 1947 Kerouac set out to look, and six years later he sat down and wrote On the Road. Half a century after it was published I am setting out on the same journey, with the same book in my backpack, the book that left its mark on spiritual paths far more than on the trains, trucks, hitched rides and other means of transport Kerouac made use of.

Kerouac coined the term Beat Generation — a generation of wild, unrestrained writers, in life as in writing. As a teenager I used to sneak out, on their inspiration, to the edge of some street and put my thumb up to get anywhere at all, hoping to be back the same night so I could get up for first period.

When I arrived in San Francisco I stayed at the Green Tortoise, a hostel a little way up the hill from what was, for me, the navel of the city: City Lights Book Store, the shop and publishing house that put out most of that generation's books. Next to the shop was the bar where the wild crowd was said to have sat.

The bar was remarkably quiet and the shop was half asleep. The hostel, by contrast, was boiling over. In its large art deco lobby sat a grandmother of about seventy, smoking weed out of a pipe and chatting away with a group of young people. I sat there and talked to Michael, who worked at the hostel to pay for his stay. Beatniks were nowhere to be found.

Between the Beat Generation and the hummus generation

In a café in the neighbourhood I met Yoni, who owns a hummus place in the middle of town. I asked him whether he needed staff. “I've got no work right now,” he said. “Business is weak, too. But I know someone who knows a lot of people. I'll introduce you and maybe we can sort something out for you.” The next day Yoni called. “Come out with us tonight. I'll introduce you to David, he'll help you find work, and we'll go out in the city.”

I agreed. I put on my finest, and in the evening Yoni and David turned up in high spirits in an old rust-red banger with a creaking back door. David turned out to be a tall, thin, red-haired, excitable chatterbox. “We're taking you to the best places in town,” they cried. Yoni was as round as a bowl of masabacha, and the two of them together looked like the funniest couple in the city.

They, for their part, took themselves entirely seriously, and were got up in their best: black suits with thin white pinstripes and long black wool coats. We drove to a club somewhere in the city. At the door the pair shook hands with every one of the security guards and handed out cigars. Both of them went around with a stock of cigars in their pockets and gave them out to all the staff, from the bouncer through the barmen to the manager.

They showed me off like an ornament and took me on a tour of all the rooms, shaking hands with anyone who was willing. After an hour or so, having got nowhere with the girls, they announced that they were bored. “The music here is terrible, let's go somewhere livelier. Do you like Latin music?” “I'm mad about it.”

Peggy Bundy on uppers

We set off perfumed, crossed at night the endless, beautiful bridge that leads from San Francisco to Oakland next door, and drove into a dense, grey, dark tangle of urban housing blocks. I did not understand where we were going. Blocks upon blocks looked back grimly through the car windows; the streets were empty and unlit. All kinds of horrifying scenarios began to run through my head, and I regretted the moment I had got into the car, and just then it pulled up in front of a bar strung with neon. The moment we walked in, every one of my fears was replaced by absolute astonishment.

At the far end of the bar stood a band of some twenty musicians from Mexico in white cowboy hats, and on the small floor dozens of couples were dancing. “Daaaviiiid! ¿Cómo estás mi amor?” shouted a woman who looked like Peggy Bundy on uppers. She was wearing leggings, a tight leopard-print top over them, and in the middle of it a big, glittering belt.

Her hair was five times the size of her head and bleached all the way through; under the heavy make-up you could see that she was past middle age. She went over to David and said an affectionate stream of Spanish words to him, and David answered with a fluent stream of his own. A minute later we were all sitting at the bar with a margarita in hand. The Spanish Bundy barely spoke English, so we communicated mostly in smiles. She asked, with a trace of jealousy, whether David and I were together. When I said we were not, she jumped off her stool and said the two Spanish words I knew: ¡Vamos a bailar!

Translator's notes

The Kerouac epigraph has been rendered back into English from the Hebrew edition quoted in the article, so the wording will not match Kerouac's own.

The putz is הפוץ, Yiddish by way of Hebrew slang, kept as it stands.

Masabacha is מסבחה, the loose, whole-chickpea hummus. The comparison is to the shape of the bowl.
