Horror Films and an Addiction to Gasoline: Indigenous Life in Canada's North

סרטי אימה והתמכרות לדלק: החיים של הילידים בצפון קנדה

Osnat Ita Skoblinski · Haaretz, Montréal, je t'aime · 1 February 2018

I kissed the Arctic Circle on my visit to Yellowknife in the Canadian North last October. I went as far north as latitude 62, not bad, considering that in the same year I also made it to minus 46 at the southern tip of New Zealand. What is astonishing is that despite the 108 degrees separating the two places, the human and historical experiences are not very different. The isolation, the snow, the hard conditions, the colonial abuse, the brutal discrimination against Indigenous populations. British politeness mixed with the coarseness of people who live on the last frontier of human settlement, at the mercy of the land and the weather.

A hundred and fourteen thousand people live in Arctic Canada, which is made up of the Northwest Territories, Yukon and Nunavut. About twenty thousand of them live in Yellowknife, a city that has always drawn workers to the mines in its area. Last October I was invited to present a project at the city's international film festival. I landed at the airport after a nine-hour flight from Montreal and was met by a stuffed polar bear and by Vinst, a Parisian carpenter who had moved to the city to work at the nearby diamond mine. “A change and an adventure,” he answered when I asked what had brought him there of all places.

In the week I spent in the city I watched dozens of local films, music performances and dance. Fewer people live in the whole region than in the town of Rehovot, and still the quantity of culture it produces is hard to fathom. Jay Bulckaert, for instance, founded the Dead North film festival, in which residents are given two months to make a horror film. “People here have nothing to do, so they're happy to make films.” I told a Canadian friend about the local industry, about the artists' collective, about the organisation Western Arctic Moving Pictures, about the excellent films and the local throat-singing performances. My friend was impressed but not amazed; he too came from a small town, and he too had seen how boredom can be destructive and lead teenagers to alcohol and to sniffing gasoline, but can also be channelled into an inexhaustible reservoir of creative work.

One of the most impressive women I met at the festival, Jerri Thrasher, made a film about two sisters from her community in the North. One of the sisters was exiled from the community and had to survive alone in the harsh conditions; the second went out to look for her. The film drew interest at the Berlinale, where it was screened. Despite the international success, Thrasher feels her film does not get enough attention in her own region. “People don't want me saying out loud what goes on in these communities, they don't want us taking it outside. But I won't be quiet. I will go on working with the communities and I will go on telling their story,” she says.

On my second day in the city I went out to the local museum, and by the frozen lake at the foot of it I met five local teenagers. They were a little drunk and noisy; one of them was even trying to ice-fish without a rod. They gathered around me with complete concentration. It isn't every day they meet somebody from Israel. They taught me how to say thank you in their language (mahsi cho). I was struck by the fact that three of them spoke different languages, according to the small communities they had come from. According to the local museum, three hundred languages were once spoken in Canada; today only fifty-eight of them remain, and they say that in another fifty years barely ten will be left. The struggle to preserve Indigenous culture and community is one of the most important things in Canada's heritage, after years in which the Canadian authorities committed crimes that threatened to erase them. Children were taken to Christian residential schools where they were forbidden to speak their language. At least six thousand children died in those schools. Hundreds of women disappeared or were murdered, and the communities continue to demand an effective investigation into those cases. Dogs that pulled traditional sleds were slaughtered, and the list goes on. In Canada's favour I will say that it is trying to atone for the past through official apologies and commissions of inquiry. More than other countries do for the Indigenous communities within their borders, and still far from ideal.

Rose, one girl in the group, struck up a conversation with me. Despite the alcohol fumes she stayed sharp and interesting. I would say of her that “she has a lot of potential,” if that sentence didn't make me nauseous. She wants to be a doctor or a dietitian, and nonetheless she is downing a bottle of vodka at noon instead of being at school. Not so different from my own high-school days in Rehovot, and still, the scale of alcoholism and addiction in these communities is beyond comprehension. “How do you get out of that cycle?” I ask later of every member of an Indigenous Canadian community (don't you dare say Indians) who has managed to get out of it.

Terry, a young anthropologist who works at the local history museum, says the education system labelled him mentally deficient, and an alcoholic, as the son of an alcoholic mother. “I am very stubborn,” he answers my question. Terry was accepted to university, and one day there a woman jumped on him in an embrace. Terry, a correct and unsmiling man, asked her with firm politeness to get off him; she explained to him that she was there that day because of him. A few years earlier, when she had asked how to succeed as he had, he had told her that if the teachers said she was fine, that her progress was fine, she shouldn't believe them. That she should always ask them where she could be more than fine, that she should take private lessons and work on the places where she was less than excellent. She followed his instructions, and because of them she too was accepted into higher education.

In the years I worked with at-risk teenagers in Israel, I always thought about that range between the great lie of telling children “you can be whatever you want” and the statistics showing that the chance of climbing the socio-economic stairs is not large. The chance of somebody like Rose becoming a doctor is not large at the moment, but you have to aim her at an attainable goal so that she doesn't lose hope. Terry thinks the solution is to draw inspiration from the Finnish education system, which directs children into vocational studies in their early teens. “But we're in Canada,” he sighs.

Part of the communities' struggle, he says, is to gain autonomy over cultural and educational institutions, but there are many internal political difficulties at this point between communities, such as one community not recognising the history of another community in the same region, for instance. Added to that difficulty is an anger at Canadian cultural trampling so great that it leads residents to reject the Canadian education system and core curriculum entirely, on the grounds that these are not their ways. “We try again and again to formulate our relationship with the Canadian authorities. Eleven treaties were signed; my grandmother was present at the signing of one of them. We want to reformulate the treaties according to their spirit, because something went wrong along the way.”

I did not expect to see such a diverse population in the far North. The Indigenous community makes up 52 per cent of the population of Arctic Canada, and includes Aboriginal people, Inuit and Métis (of mixed Aboriginal and European descent). There are quite a few immigrants in the city, and the streets are flooded with thousands of tourists from Japan and China who come to watch the northern lights, which break out almost every night in autumn and spring. There is something cosmopolitan about the small city, and still, at the film festival and the cultural events, the crowd was largely white, even though much of the content revolved around Indigenous tradition and local heroes. At the weekend, at the end of a stiff gala, I went out with the festival organisers and other guests to the Gold Range pub, where a local band played two-step and all the communities of every age mixed together on the dance floor in couples. The northern lights may be the famous attraction of the place, but for me an experience no less interesting was watching white people, Indigenous people, mine workers, hipsters and Japanese tourists dancing country dances in a bar that looked as though a fight was about to break out in it any second. The next day we tried to recover from the hangover at a local diner that serves buffalo meat, bison and muskox — a sort of prehistoric cow, unique on the face of the earth. “Somebody from a local community put up footage of himself hunting a whale on Facebook and was attacked by animal rights activists,” Terry told me. “They don't understand that this is an ancient practice in which families hunted one whale in order to supply food for their family for a year. How do you explain to white people things that operate out of a completely different world from the one they live in?”

Terry's words echoed the encounter I had with Māori in New Zealand. As in Canada, the Indigenous people there were dispossessed of their lands and their customs. There too many communities suffer from poverty and addiction. There too the government is trying to mend its ways in a manner that does not satisfy the communities. In New Zealand the word Pākehā is commonly used for non-Māori. I liked the fact that it was precisely the non-Indigenous who got a collective name, turning them, by a terminological trick, into the “other.” If you insist on taking a side, you can say that white people and colonial history only harmed Indigenous peoples around the world, but that would be a one-dimensional picture. A comprehensive book on the history of New Zealand describes how most species of plants and animals there were wiped out with human settlement. You can relate to that as a disaster that struck the ecology, but you can also see human beings and what they brought with them as part of the ecology, as part of the natural process of the world. Throughout history communities have wandered from place to place and created ecological changes or started culture wars. Today too we go on moving and shifting, partly from lack of choice and partly out of a search for a better life or an adventure. Today too we take the lands of neighbouring or distant communities, or flee wars that destroy our own.

You cannot reverse great movements of population, but neither can you go on living with an inequality that tears states and communities apart. A friend of mine took a DNA test to clarify her heritage and was surprised to discover that alongside her Jewish-Maghrebi roots, her saliva sample revealed countries like Nigeria. In her opinion every child in the world should take a test like that, in order to understand that we are all mixed, that we all come from a thousand places, that family and local heritage has meaning but in the end it is only one way of looking at things. At the end of the day, when we look up at the northern lights, it doesn't matter where we or our parents are from. Our breath is taken away.
