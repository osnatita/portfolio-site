On the Sanctity, the Trauma and the Sexism of Music Festivals

על הקדושה, הטראומה והסקסיזם של פסטיבלי מוזיקה

Osnat Ita Skoblinski · Pop Montreal, September 2016

1

We stopped going to churches and synagogues. In a sense the music venues — festivals, clubs and performance spaces — are our spiritual and communal centres, our houses of faith. We crowd together in the brotherhood of fans, we are moved together, we let the high priests on the stage speak to us. We sing with them, dance with them, give them what we would give no other stranger: we surrender to what they give us.

2

Alongside the elevation, music festivals are a slightly traumatic experience. Our reality, which takes place constantly and in parallel in the online and offline worlds, creates a serious fear of missing out. We express interest in dozens of Facebook events we will not attend, we are constantly exposed to documentation of other people's experiences, and we want to show that here, we too are living life. That fear intensifies into a continuous anxiety at big festivals. For every band you see, you miss five and sometimes ten others. And FOMO can take the reins of consciousness without warning and ruin the moment. A few years ago I went to a big music festival in Barcelona with a friend who suffers, as I do, from a low threshold of concentration and a lack of patience. We spent twenty minutes at each performance before moving on — even when several of our favourite artists were playing in front of us. It is a little ironic that the fear of missing out makes us miss what is right in front of our eyes. Music festivals, then, are an excellent opportunity to practise zen, staying in the moment, even when the moment is a psychedelic metal set.

3

Last weekend Montreal held Pop Montreal, a local festival that included four hundred events across five days, from morning until night. The programme included panels of musicians and artists, picnics, activities for children and, of course, performances in every possible genre — from John Cage in a spoken-word show, through John Cale in moving ballads, to the cult band Psychic TV. Despite the scale of the production it is a fairly intimate festival; many of the performances take place in small spaces, in neighbourhood bars, and a lot of them are made up of sets by local musicians who draw a loyal following. Montreal has excellent performances every evening anyway, so in a sense the festival not only creates a temporary new arena but amplifies the existing scene and exposes local musicians to visitors from outside.

4

I spent the festival's opening night at a performance by the blues-rock outfit The Kills, led by the charismatic pair Alison Mosshart and Jamie Hince. Mosshart came on stage and hypnotised the audience as though she were Blondie in a glam metal band. It was hard to take your eyes off her dancing, twitching, falling, throwing her blonde hair in every direction, flirting with the audience and recoiling from it. The audience enjoyed it; I less so. The Kills' music is pop in the disguise of rock: energetic, catchy, enjoyable. There is nothing wrong with that, but it isn't especially interesting either. Call me a masochist, but I like performances that abuse me a little, that challenge my eardrum and my thinking. After twenty minutes I began to be bored, I knew there was another interesting performance not far away, and the thought of missing it would not let go. I left and went into the museum of modern art to watch Jerusalem In My Heart. It was the best decision I made that weekend.

5

Radwan Ghazi Moumneh sat on a chair on a stage in the dark room and opened with beautiful, powerful Arabic singing. He was dressed in black and his eyes were hidden behind dark sunglasses. Behind him, frantic abstract images were projected onto two overlapping screens. His voice is magnificent, wounding. The music behind it was electronic, monotonous, industrial; the feeling forebodes ill. This is music of tension and trauma. It was hard to see him except in the moments when the flashes from the projector fell on his head. At times he left off singing, plucked a buzuq or blew into microphones that create different textures of sound. The projections behind him kept changing. On either side of the stage lay two women in black. Only in the last song did they raise their upper bodies and shake their hair in circular movements, half a nod to metal, half a traditional belly-dance motion. Behind them were projected figures of women performing the same movement.

Jerusalem In My Heart is the experimental music project of the Lebanese-Canadian artist Radwan Ghazi Moumneh, a respected music producer and studio owner. Moumneh has performed with the project for close to a decade. Each of his performances is a one-off work combining the visual and the sonic. Only after eight years of one-off performances did Moumneh begin to release albums on the respected experimental label Constellation Records. For several months a year he works out of Beirut, and he produced one of Mashrou' Leila's albums. During the performance I thought of Yair Yona's new album Sword, which uses military radio recordings from the Yom Kippur War and offers it an alternative soundtrack. Both these artists create a connection of trauma and feeling, of delicate layers that develop out of a gentle electronic monotony that forms fertile, unifying ground for musical elements from different and even opposing places. In a sense both create a connection between the local and the universal from two sides of a border, and it is interesting to listen to them close together.

6

The audience in Montreal is generous; it can listen to experimental music for hours in complete attention. One of the most legendary bands in the city is Godspeed You! Black Emperor. In the nineties they were part of the group that shaped Montreal as a music and culture centre. They held a performance and workshop space and played there once a month to raise money for the rent. They say a performance of theirs is an experience every local or visitor has to go through. It is hard to call it a performance — it is really an event of consciousness. The nine-piece ensemble always performs with projections, and the music is monotonous, slow, repetitive and hypnotic. It builds slowly into peaks; sometimes that takes five minutes, sometimes twenty. Sometimes the wall of sound is so heavy that it is almost impossible to contain any longer and the eyes wander over the projections, looking for consolation. They played four consecutive evenings outside the festival programme and every ticket sold out in advance. On the evening I was there they redesigned the consciousness of an audience that stood pressed against one another without moving, in a large church converted into a performance space. The confession booths at the sides were closed, but there was no need of them: the congregation of believers surrendered completely and came out two hours later, exhausted and elevated.

7

Three musicians and producers come up on stage at an afternoon panel in the festival headquarters. One of them comes up with a modular synthesiser, an oscillator, a sequencer and effects pedals. They talk about the production process and about how the world treats them in a very male industry. “For many years I was a geek and taught myself how to make beats. I didn't think of it as something special, I didn't think about being a woman, about being queer, until I put out an album and went out into the world. I was once called in to take part in a hip-hop production. I was the only woman in the room and I felt that between the bro atmosphere I was pushed to the side. In the end one of them turned to me and asked me to sing a bridge. I refused.” — the musician Marie-Hélène Delorme, who performs as Foxtrott, shared her experiences with the audience. The other two creators nod. Annie Sama, who brought the equipment and performs as APigeon, goes on: “Sometimes journalists treat me as a singer and I correct them and tell them I make my own beats. ‘Are you sure?' they ask me, and it comes from men and women alike.” Meryem Saci, a member of the hip-hop group Nomadic Massive, adds: “I was always the only girl in hip-hop ensembles. I felt I was special, that I was the only one the men in the room respected because I could sing and make music. I thought, here I am, I'm better than other women, I get to hang out with the boys. It took me time to understand that you have to resist that feeling, that I want and need to find more creative and strong women.” I wish that one day a panel of women producers will not be a special occasion, and that in a conversation like that women producers will talk only about their creative process and not about coping in a male world. Until then, Sama sums it up with excellent advice: “Don't hesitate to approach whoever inspires you, and don't hesitate to walk into an electronic music equipment shop and say, hey — I have no experience but I'm interested in trying this.”

8

In another room, an hour later, at the other end of the city, a panel took place that also dealt with difference inside a territory dominated by men: the techno scene. The audience is excited, because the guest of honour is appearing for the first time in her female persona, RROSE. RROSE is one of the stage names of the electronic musician Seth Horvitz, who until now has performed under the name SUTEKH. Horvitz makes excellent experimental electronic music and has released albums on respected labels. She comes on stage as RROSE, a name taken from Marcel Duchamp's female persona. She is wearing a long black dress, a black wig and lipstick, and a certain resemblance to her source of inspiration is evident. “I thought I was retiring from the world of techno,” she says. “I went to study music and thought that from then on I'd be doing composition; I didn't want to be another white nerdy guy playing techno on the floor. There are a lot of problematic things in the techno world. When I was playing around the world they'd take me to dinners where there were only techno geeks and maybe one girl, a friend of one of them, who wouldn't speak. At university I started making techno again with one of my professors, and over time I came to understand that I wanted to go out with this material under a different persona, and that's how RROSE was created. It's a wonderful experience talking to my daughters about RROSE. Explaining to them why dad is sometimes a woman. They decided that as RROSE I'm allowed the privileges of girls.”

Patti Schmidt, the interviewer and an organiser of the MUTEK music festival, says that when she saw her perform at Berghain, which she calls “the macho temple of techno,” she was astonished at how much the figure of RROSE at the decks had an anti-machoistic effect on the room, and went on to ask how the new persona is received in clubs, in places like Berghain. “I'm sometimes astonished that people come up to me after a set and treat me as a man, in bro talk, except that now there aren't the usual backslaps I used to get after sets. People come up to me who wouldn't have approached me otherwise, and on top of that I have access to new places; the excellent collective Discwoman, which is exclusive to female electronic artists, invited me to take part in their line-up. That's an enormous honour for me.” Schmidt goes on to ask about feminine elements in the music she makes, and RROSE answers with a wonderful description: “I want my music to be aggressive in a seductive way, the kind that comes at you without your expecting it, on the threshold between pleasure and pain. To build something that builds slowly into something so intense that it almost hurts, and you don't know how you got there in the first place.”

9

If music festivals and clubs are our modern temples, it is time to do some housecleaning in them, and to look carefully at where the sexism sits and how it can be kicked out. We can start by actively seeking out women producers and creators, by making sure that festivals have no fewer women on the stages and on the technical equipment than men. We can start by simply being decent human beings, respecting the personal space of everyone willing to spend their money and their time on an evening in a club or a performance space, and refusing to take part in any space or organisation that settles for less than that minimum.
