I Hate Montreal — the Best City in the World

אני שונאת את מונטריאול - העיר הכי טובה בעולם

Osnat Ita Skoblinski · Haaretz, Montréal, je t'aime · 17 January 2018

Montreal is like a neighbourhood with a perfect degree of gentrification. Prices are still cheap, the neighbourhood is still fairly shabby and very communal, but it already has every ingredient that makes a creative young urbanite drool.

A big, airy three-bedroom apartment in a central location, with a large storage room, goes for around 3,300 shekels; students can find a room for a thousand. Next to my house there is a large park with a completely free swimming pool, skating areas and playgrounds. Not far from it spreads the coveted Mile End, which, like every urban cliché, is full of cafés, record shops, bookshops, bars and a sense of separateness and self-importance. I don't want to write this piece. In fact I have been avoiding it for more than three years. Why would I write that I live in a city that is the best-kept secret in North America? Why would I tell you how good it is here? I don't want you dreaming of living here, because part of what makes this city so wonderful is the way it scrapes along under the radar. People in the know understand what it is worth. The general public has no idea. So instead I'll tell you why I hate Montreal.

I hate Montreal because it produces an impossible sense of missing out, accompanied by a dulling of the senses. The world-famous jazz festival, the biggest comedy festival in North America, film festivals, horror festivals, a festival of Arab culture or African culture, avant-garde dance, food, street art. Sometimes it seems there are ten festivals running at any given moment, and every one of them puts on free events for the public. Along with the good bands that keep coming through, the feeling is that it is impossible to enjoy everything the city has to offer. In Israel a famous artist turns up once in a while, everyone goes, the experience is collective and you can go home euphoric. Here it is endless, and you can always pop down to New York, seven hours away. In the last three months alone I saw Roger Waters, LCD Soundsystem, Courtney Barnett and Kurt Vile, and that is a very partial list.

I hate Montreal because I no longer have any way of taking my anger out on strangers. In Israel there is no shortage of people to let off steam at: the woman who pushed in ahead of you in the queue, the man who cut you off on the road, the one who decided to ask why you aren't smiling, as though you were a walking toothpaste advert. Here people are polite to the point of insanity. More than once I have got into a courteous argument in which each side insisted the other take the right of way, an argument that seemed like it would have gone on for hours if I hadn't simply given in and gone first. Once a woman was cross with me for not telling her she was walking in front of me and slowing me down, immediately after she had apologised, of course. Nobody throws sexist remarks at me, nobody tries it on. On the occasions when my dress decides to be a shirt above my bike, or the buttons on my shirt go on strike, not only does nobody say anything, nobody so much as looks in my direction, and so I get home and discover I have ridden ten kilometres partly naked. I can't shout at anyone, not even at terrible service staff. There is no putting anybody in their place here. Out of sheer frustration I took up boxing, where I met quite a few friends from the Middle East.

I hate Montreal because it forces me to stand behind my feminist values. Before I moved here I didn't know how to do simple things like drill into a wall, install a dimmer, fix a bike. I could whine that my father never taught me because I'm a woman, and in the same breath call him or my brothers to help me. Here there's nobody to talk to. I went into a bike shop and the guy working there said, “Oh, that's an easy repair, you can do it yourself, I'll sell you the part.” When I didn't look enthusiastic he added, “If you get stuck, go to the bike maintenance co-op, there are two in the neighbourhood, they'll help you.” In personal life, too, all this gender equality generates a great deal of work. The Frenchwomen who have moved here complain constantly about the lack of male attention. Nobody makes a move here, not in the street and not in a bar. If you want someone, the task falls to you to pick your victim and let him know, and if you are expecting him to kiss you in a moment of passion you will probably go on waiting; the most you are likely to get out of him is a request for permission. So not only do you have to start it, you have to initiate the kiss as well. So much work.

I hate Montreal because it won't let me sit quietly at home. I thought days of minus twenty would be the perfect excuse for burrowing under the duvet daily, but this damned city gives me no rest. In the central park that grows out of a hill in the middle of the city there are endless cross-country ski trails and a big lake that turns into a skating rink. You can get to the park by bus, which voids every excuse. Equipment can be rented without any trouble. A short drive out of the city and there are endless parks, mountains, lakes, rivers and streams begging you to come and visit. I hadn't been out in nature since my school days, and suddenly I have skis, skates, a snowboard, snowshoes and hiking boots in the house. I don't know who I am any more.

I hate Montreal because I no longer have an excuse for creative inactivity. There is too much money here for art and culture. I can't wave around sentences like “artists can't live here,” or “if only somebody supported my art I'd make wonderful things.” There are government funds for culture and art, from Canada and from Quebec both, and many of them take a progressive line. The film fund announced that fifty per cent of the projects it finances would be projects by women. You can't get rich off the funds, but you can certainly make work. This is a perfect city for people who want to create and live at a comfortable standard. Conditions are so comfortable for artists here that far too much mediocre art gets made; the bar to entry is lower. It is a city that lets people realise their potential, including people whose potential is mediocre.

What do I like here? The impossible frost, and the challenge of the language. Life is boring when you don't have to learn anything, and here I am not bored for a moment, because I have to give my free time to learning French, or to learning how to cope with minus twenty-five. Every day I learn again how to walk on ice without slipping, and, when you do slip, what the safest way to fall is. Since I got a car I have developed ice-driving skills and impressive arm muscles, from all the snow clearing and the digging around the wheels that goes with every exit from a parking space. French speakers will pretend not to understand English; they will assume you haven't bothered, out of anglophone arrogance, to learn the local language. But the moment you show some command of it they will be impressed, they will compliment you, and then decide they haven't the energy for a conversation in your crooked French, and switch immediately to their crooked English. And so every attempt at a conversation in French dies just after the ça va bien.

I also like the mediocrity of the city. This is not a city with ambitions. It has already been the biggest city in Canada, and it seems that was enough for it. The locals abandoned the church en masse in what is called the Quiet Revolution, but they seem to find it hard to shake off Catholic modesty. You don't come here to make it big or to conquer the world. You come here to live well, to get a year of parental leave at X per cent of your salary that the two of you can split between you. You come here because the public transport is good and the bus driver will stop for you anywhere along the route after dark, because healthcare is free and the universities are cheap. You come here because the revulsion at the church and its values removed the social pressure to marry or to have children. You come here because you can breathe, and it is terribly boring, breathing all the time. Forget it. Shit city.
