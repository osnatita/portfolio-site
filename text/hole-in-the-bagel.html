A Hole in the Bagel

חור בבייגל

Osnat Ita Skoblinski · Montreal, 2016

The largest Jewish community in Canada built food institutions in Montreal that became an inseparable part of the city. On a special tour, a food historian takes residents past the sandwich exploding with smoked meat and the crisp, faintly sweet bagel, straight into the colourful history of one of the most fascinating Jewish communities in North America. Immigration, family recipes, entrepreneurship and a fondness for gambling run through the chronicles of the city's eating places, which serve a mixed population of Hasidim and hipsters, locals and tourists.

In Europe people believed a bagel could ward off evil spirits, and pregnant women used to wear one around the neck. It is not clear whether this means a single bagel on a string or a whole necklace of them, and it is even less clear how they resisted the temptation to eat it. The bagel is one of the most famous foods in Montreal. It is so Montreal that many residents have no idea it emigrated here from Poland with the Jewish community. The Montreal bagel, smaller than its New York brother, is made from coins of yeast dough with malt, boiled in water and honey and baked in an oven. It is very crisp outside, airy inside, and slightly sweet.

The city's two famous bagel bakeries compete for customers in fashionable Mile End, where two bearded populations live in relative coexistence: Hasidim and hipsters. St-Viateur and Fairmount both have roots in the early twentieth century. Every self-respecting Montrealer has a preferred bakery, though the truth is that in blind tastings they insist on a difference they cannot quite locate. They serve bagels fresh from the oven twenty-four hours a day, three hundred and sixty-five days a year. At weekends the queues spill into the street, and in the middle of the night it is not unusual to see a group of young people devouring a dozen bagels straight out of the oven alongside cream cheese and smoked salmon, with a drunk's appetite that leaves trails of sesame and cream cheese on their cheeks.

From the shtetl to Anthony Bourdain

“Behind the Bagel” is the name of a tour through the history of Jewish food in Montreal, started by Kat Romanow, a food researcher and historian. Three years ago Romanow and her partner, Sydney Warshaw, launched the pop-up catering outfit The Wandering Chew, through which they run Jewish culinary events. Their meals serve the less familiar dishes of world Jewry, like the Mexican community, which, which produced mutations like gefilte fish in a hot Mexican sauce. Romanow started the “Behind the Bagel” food tour under the roof of Montreal's new Jewish museum, in order to tell the story of the richness of the Jewish kitchen and to look at local history through the prism of its eating places: the ones still working and the ones that survive only in the memories of the people who lived here. Researching the tour, Romanow interviewed at least twenty people: past and present business owners, and long-standing members of the community who could describe the daily life of the community to her and how the eating places fitted into it.

Montreal is a city with a palpable Jewish presence, both in the size of the community and in its influence on local food and culture. The Jewish community here was the largest in Canada, but after Quebec began its push for independence and for the dominance of francophone culture, anglophones, many of them Jews, were afraid of waking up one morning in a new country ruled by a language they did not speak, and left en masse for Toronto and other cities. Today about a hundred thousand Jews live in Montreal. As during the great immigration before the Second World War, many of them settled in Mile End and along St-Laurent, and the city's Jewish culinary institutions are still there; among their customers you could sometimes spot Leonard Cohen, perhaps the community's best-known son.

The history of Jewish communal life is well documented in the books of Mordecai Richler, one of the most important Canadian writers and one of the famous Jewish voices of North America. His novel The Apprenticeship of Duddy Kravitz was also adapted into a successful film of the same name. That book, and the film, immortalised the workers' restaurant Wilensky's, founded by Moe Wilensky in 1932. The family says Moe's father opened a barbershop down Fairmount and his son sold snacks and sandwiches in it. One day the son bought a toaster from a salesman for $68, worth about a thousand dollars today. His father was astonished at the decision to spend so much money on an electrical appliance, but thanks to it Moe invented the “Wilensky Special,” a grilled sandwich of salami and bologna on a plain roll smeared with fluorescent mustard. The place is a time capsule of the diners of the thirties and they keep to firm principles: you cannot have it without mustard, they will never cut it in half, and the staff will not accept a tip. The celebrity chef Anthony Bourdain stops at Wilensky's on almost every visit one of his programmes makes to Montreal. It is not a culinary transcendence so much as the grilled version of a roll with sausage, but the old delicatessen, the rows of sandwiches pressed one after another in their yellow hoops, the soda syrups made on the premises, and the sense of standing inside a living food museum, are worth every penny.

There is a good deal of similarity between New York Jewish food and the Montreal kind: these are communities that came from the same places at more or less the same time. Each city adapted its own variations, but one place in Montreal keeps faith with the metropolis to the south — Cheskie's, a neighbourhood institution founded thirteen years ago. Cheskie's sells the best babkas in the city, with an unbelievable balance between dough and good chocolate, along with other Eastern European pastries in the New York Jewish style. Few people know that Cheskie's is a copy of “Shloimy's,” the bakery belonging to Cheskie's brother in Borough Park, Brooklyn. The locals don't care; this is their Montreal bakery, the one where hipsters, French bourgeois and Hasidim mix in a long queue at the end of which Filipina counter staff serve them koukosh (Russian babka), cheese crowns (an Austrian dessert) and New York black-and-white cookies. Montreal at its best.

A Romanian paradise

After babkas, bagels and a grilled sausage sandwich, the tour climbs down St-Laurent, known as The Main, and lets us burn off a little of the Jewish carbohydrate bombs we have dropped into our stomachs. Romanow points out places where businesses that have closed once stood: the big market where they used to slaughter chickens on the spot; the St-Laurent bakery where, according to one witness, the staff were “stern Eastern European women who could have thrown the average man across a boxing ring.” The bakery supplied bread to the steakhouse opposite, Moishes, which began life under the tempting name “Romanian Paradise.” Moishe, who worked at the Paradise, won the restaurant in a poker game in 1938, and changed its name when Romania joined the Nazi axis. It is an old-world institution, and Moishes is regularly ranked among the best steak restaurants in the world. The meat is served alongside rye bread, chopped liver and Romanian aubergine salad. In photographs from the past you can see Jewish families in their finest clothes arriving to ascend to Moishe's Romanian paradise on special occasions.

Romanow leads us around the corner into a back alley, and this is the most moving moment of the tour. There is no bustling restaurant there and no trace of an old bakery, only a cramped lane and a building with balconies from which laundry spills into electrical wires. “This is what it looked like back then,” she says. New immigrants lived here at high density and everyone spilled into the street because there was no room in the house. While she speaks, a van comes through the little alley and pushes us to the side; we try not to fall into the potholes. Inside the crowding, the discomfort and the charm of the Montreal spring, you can imagine for a moment how this street felt and smelled when it was full of a jumble of Yiddish, Russian, Romanian, English and French. We stop in front of a small gate. “Mrs Whyte lived here,” she says, referring to a well-known pickle brand. In the thirties Mrs Whyte persuaded her husband, who delivered bread, to offer the pickles she made as well. He was sceptical; everyone made their own pickles in those days, but the business became a success and a brand that has survived to this day. “On Halloween there was a long queue outside Mrs Whyte's gate, where instead of handing out sweets they handed out a pickle in a bag,” Romanow reads from one of the testimonies, and hands a fat pickle to every member of the group.

The Jewish community left its mark not only through pickles, meat and pastries. It changed the way people shopped in Quebec. “This is where the Jewish grocery that changed how people bought food stood,” Romanow says, pointing to a street corner that housed Steinberg's, a small grocery opened in 1917 that by 1934 had become the first modern supermarket in Quebec and grew into a large chain. In those years business in Montreal was conducted in English; the anglophones owned the businesses and the francophones worked for them. The five Steinberg brothers understood the potential of addressing the francophone majority, insisted on bilingual staff and bilingual signage, and became so popular a chain that in local French the expression “to do a Steinberg” became a substitute for “to do the shopping.”

From a historic smoked sandwich to a modern rugelach

We close the tour at the most famous restaurant of them all, Schwartz's, which serves smoked meat sandwiches and always has enormous peppered cuts of beef lying in its window. Outside Schwartz's a long queue of customers stretches even in winter, when the temperature bungee-jumps into the region of minus thirty. They are willing to queue for two thin slices of bread that explode with rich, juicy, fat-dripping meat, properly cured in garlic, pepper and other spices before smoking. It is a less sweet version of the New York pastrami sandwich. The meat at Schwartz's is cured, smoked and steamed according to a Romanian recipe that Reuben Schwartz brought with him in the nineteen-twenties.

Like his neighbour Moishe, Reuben was fond of gambling, and in 1932 he had to sell the business to his friend the musician Maurice, who threw famous parties in the restaurant after closing time. Maurice left the restaurant to his carer, who sold it to the establishment's accountant, who went by the marvellous name Hy Diamond. Diamond in turn sold it to a group of partners that included Céline Dion. That last handover of the keys caused great anxiety that changes would be made to the historic institution, but apart from a few dishes added to the menu the place kept its character. A real Montrealer will always order a sandwich there, a pickle and a cream soda.

The Jewish community no longer lives up St-Laurent, or in Mile End; it has moved to the outlying neighbourhoods of Côte-des-Neiges and Côte Saint-Luc, and the libraries, synagogues and kosher restaurants moved with it. Montreal's Jewish food institutions became part of the city, consolidated a local identity and kept their distinctiveness, as in many North American cities. There is more immigrant nostalgia in them than contemporary Jewish identity, and they are an inseparable part of Montreal's DNA. Only one place is trying to renew the tradition. At the bakery Hof Kelsten, Jeffrey Finkelstein, a young chef who came home after a tour of respected kitchens around the world, serves a crisp and heavenly rugelach alongside challah, shakshuka and kneidlach soup. The bakery and café are part of the “new Jewish kitchen” trend, which makes the finest, most up-to-date version of the tastiest recipes from the best ingredients. Yes, it is pretentious, but Finkelstein pulls it off. The bakery supplies bread to the city's most admired restaurants, including Moishes and the celebrated Joe Beef. Hof Kelsten is a new Jewish kitchen that may hint at the future of Jewish food in Montreal and beyond.
