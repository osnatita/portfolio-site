How I Fell in Love with Neil Young, Got Permission to Sleep with Jane Birkin, and Got Tangled Up with the Mafia

איך התאהבתי בניל יאנג, קיבלתי רשות לשכב עם ג׳יין בירקין והסתבכתי עם המאפיה

Osnat Ita Skoblinski · Haaretz, Montréal, je t'aime · 29 August 2018 · Part one

There are a few experiences you have to go through in order to pass the Canadian trial by fire and call yourself a local: getting stuck on a street corner where you and a stranger try to give each other the right of way and end up in an infinite loop; pouring maple sap fresh from the tree onto ice so you can roll it onto a lolly stick and lick it with pleasure; walking home with a bin bag full of live lobsters because the season has just opened; digging your car out of a mountain of snow in the morning; and, of course, seeing Neil Young live.

Unfortunately, and to the great disappointment of many of my friends, I never especially warmed to Neil Young. I thought he was a brilliant songwriter and I fell in love with a few covers of his songs, but something in that high, grating voice scraped at my ear. I got used to Bob Dylan's sandpaper early; perhaps there are tastes that are hard to acquire later. Still, when I was invited to see Neil Young live I did not refuse. Who am I to refuse to watch a living legend. “Really?” asked every one of my Canadian friends, eyes shining, when I told them my plans for the coming summer. “Wow, you don't understand, this man is the soundtrack to such significant moments in my life.” It made no difference whether they were fifty or thirty; they all said they used to listen to him while passing a joint around in high school. Neil Young is the soundtrack of Canada. A consensus.

If there is one thing you do not want to say at a Neil Young concert in Canada, it is “I don't like Neil Young,” out loud, at exactly the moment a song ends. In one second I felt thousands of pairs of eyes fix on me, ready to impale me on a maple leaf. “I do now, I do now,” I added immediately, so they would lower the crimson spears. I wasn't lying. In the run-up to the concert I was given a playlist of selected songs by one of the loves of my life. I listened to them for months. Then I read his biography. I wanted to die, but more than that I wanted to hug him. And then I saw him live. We sat at the foot of the hill, underneath us — fittingly, for the godfather of grunge — a blanket in a plaid-shirt pattern, in our hands a bad Canadian beer, and on stage Young splitting the sky open and filling our eyes with tears. Fifteen minutes of […], an encore of the heartbreaking Harvest Moon, and the guitar work of a virtuoso whose heart has been broken too many times. I came out a little sentimental, but it's Neil Young, so it's allowed.

At seventy-three his voice still sounds exactly as it sounded at twenty-three, which is the luck of someone born with a thousand-year-old soul. The man has been through countless lifetimes: the death of a close friend, a son with cerebral palsy, divorce, a disabling stroke, wide-ranging musical searches, and disappointing fans who refused to accept his changes of direction and wanted variations on the same album over and over. In the months I spent listening to him deeply, I was struck by Neil Young's enormous capacity to tear into a guitar, to write the perfect ballad, or to try to communicate with his son through new electronic technologies — and still to sound like Neil Young.

As far as I was concerned, at that point I could have gone home. The concert filled me completely. Only the week before I had seen Herbie Hancock, and there is a limit to the number of living legends one can absorb in an allotted time. But we had come to the Quebec summer festival, where you can see dozens of artists for a few hundred shekels, and there were more performances on the programme. The next day, after a touristic brunch in the revolving restaurant at the top of the hotel and a visit to the lovely old town, we went out to see Jane Birkin. I had always liked Birkin: for the style, for the bag, for Serge. But I had no idea how magnetic her charisma was. I stood a few metres away from her and watched her lean on the piano with a nonchalance that cannot be taught. She wore an elegant suit and did not forget to slip in a pair of Converse, so that we would not accidentally forget that despite the fragility of her voice, she is the embodiment of voice. I did not expect it, but Birkin destroyed me, opened a crack in my heart and filled it with sparkling wine. I looked at her, seventy-one and as hot as she ever was. Her talk with the audience, the precision of the performance and the feline movements consoled me: sex appeal has nothing to do with age. Jane Birkin makes old age look unthreatening. In fact Jane Birkin raised my humidity levels. I looked at her and understood — I have a crush, and better women than me have had one there. I looked at the love of my life and asked, “May I?” — “Jane Birkin? Yes, she's fully covered by your celebrity exemption.” That permission produced an unaccountable satisfaction. But it is only fair: the love of my life has permission to sleep with Grace Jones.

I had to leave before the big hits; a journalist friend had invited me to the golden ring of the golden ring: the production box, with the best view and alcohol for pennies. I walked for a quarter of an hour, but they were giant steps, because I had in fact arrived in another world. Tens of thousands of kids filled the park for Lil Yachty, drunk and at varying degrees of psychoactive influence. They moved in tight packs, boys and girls, many of them in what looked like the same outfit. The hill that the night before had watched with eyes full of tears was now filled with eyes glazed, clouded, rolled upwards. The emergency crew worked overtime and stretchers kept coming back carrying teenagers who had not taken the right drugs, or the right quantities, or had taken the summer holiday as a licence to destroy themselves. They were so thrilled by the presence of their favourite rapper that I almost wanted to give them my VIP bracelet. Almost. Lil Yachty bounced around on stage and mostly shouted hey and ho over his own songs playing in the background, not that the kids noticed the difference.

Darkness came down around nine and I made a firm decision to go out and not let the old age at the door outwit me. Since September I have gone out maybe ten times; in my wardrobe lie wide linen trousers, and what I call highlights from the sun is actually a few white hairs. So when my friend invited me to an after-party with a local hip-hop star, I agreed. I should have known it was a recipe for trouble. At the end of an excellent set by Future we followed the magnificently drunk producer to a club where I would begin the night at a private table with bottles of champagne and end it with a broken finger.
