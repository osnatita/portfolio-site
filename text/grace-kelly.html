Driving Grace Kelly to New York

Osnat Ita Skoblinski · Maine, summer 2019

I find myself in a car with Ron Maiberg, in the boot a work of art that costs about half my house, uninsured, which I packed in the remains of some cardboard, crossing Maine towards New York. A blink and I am swimming naked with a well-known artist I met on the way to the beach on a lovely island in Maine. A press of the pedal and I am in Cape Cod listening to Russian music on the shore of a lake. A week earlier I had broken down in front of the washing machine when I understood that the pile of children's clothes would never end.

2019, this is my second summer as a mother, and I need to escape somewhere and go back to my previous life. At least for a week. A year before that I moved in with the Canadian widower and his two children. The romance with the widower began when he was still separated, and the love story with the children began a month after I met him, when I got into his car. The Ramones started playing and the three-year-old and the five-year-old shouted together, “Hey ho, let's go!” with the music. Move a few years forward, add a tragic story about a mother's death, and I am a full-time mother: trying to balance mountains of laundry, good nutrition for the children, a career, and, in North America as in North America, football tournaments.

Only a year and a half earlier I had come back from a six-month journey in which I joined a storyteller's tour of Australia and New Zealand with a stop in Singapore, hitched a ride on my parents' trip to Scotland, moved in with new friends in Edinburgh, and installed myself in the flat of a mathematician friend in Paris who had gone to a conference. I had lived a whole life with the question “where is the next adventure?” blinking in front of my eyes, and here I had landed in an adventure of superhuman dimensions: parenthood.

When the Canadian saw me having a nervous breakdown in front of the washing machine, he gave me ten days. “Take the car, travel, rest.” A friend invited me to spend time on the shore of a lake in Cape Cod with her husband's childhood friends. I thought maybe I could stop on the way in Maine, at Ron Maiberg's, with whom I had been corresponding for a while about swapping flats: Maine for Montreal. I wrote to him and he came back with an offer: this may sound absurd, and feel free to say no, but I have to go to New York urgently to sell a Warhol and I need a partner for the drive. Bling-bling-bling, the blinking light that had faded from disuse came on. I almost answered immediately — obviously! But the new Ita said, with judgement, “I'll consult the Canadian and get back to you.” The next conversation I had with the Canadian was by phone, on the way to Maine. I asked him, “But what do you do if you see a moose?”

The day after Maiberg's offer I equipped the old Camry with dozens of CDs and crossed the border in a tiny town on the Quebec-Maine line like an alien, probably the first Israeli passport they had ever come across. I carried on into an enormous forest where every hundred metres somebody had bothered to install a yellow sign: “Caution — moose crossing!” After the twentieth sign I wondered to myself what you actually do if you meet a moose on the road, so I called the Canadian. They must teach it in Canadian driving schools. “Better to crash into a wall, or swerve into a ditch,” said the Canadian, in a level voice. “Hitting a moose is like hitting a semi-trailer. If you really, really have no choice, then brake and then accelerate just before the impact, so the moose flies upwards and its antlers don't come through your windscreen.” And so, equipped with these calming instructions, I carried on for another six hours until I reached the winding, enchanted coastline of Maine and Ron Maiberg's house.

Here is Grace Kelly, said Maiberg, after feeding me well. And there in the corner of the living room she stood. Not even blinking. With the eternal grace reserved for those who went young. I tried to pack her as best I could in the remains of cardboard, sellotape and bubble wrap. Patch upon patch I assembled on her a sort of grotesque mummy's costume. I photographed her before she went into the car and sent it to the Canadian. And he, who had managed countless tours and was used to expensive cargo, asked me, “That's insured, right?”

From that moment I would not agree to leave the car, so we stopped and went to the rest stops in turns. On the way we listened to the best of Dylan, Neil Young and Townes Van Zandt from his collection, and then I tried to continue the line into the present with Courtney Barnett and Kurt Vile of my own. At the New York skyline I broke the line. I had to have the soundtrack of Young Fathers in the background, and to Ron's credit it should be said that he did not say a word about the heavy bass and the hypnotic loops fired straight from Glasgow into the stereo.

At the hotel, on the Canadian's advice, I asked whether there was a safe for the painting. They looked at us and then at the enormous mummy with Grace Kelly inside it, with a puzzled but polite expression, and said no. When we understood that Kelly was not only larger than life but too large for the hotel lift, I insisted on going up with the mummy in the service lift with a member of staff. When the lift stopped on the way for an unclear reason, I imagined gangs of organised robbers stealing half a Montreal house from under my hands. Grace arrived safely in Maiberg's room. I settled into mine, and against every instinct I had, we left her behind and went to eat.

The next day we brought her with due ceremony to the gallery. There, the work I had been guarding was stripped of its shrouds and taken just like that out into the street, to the framer's next door, as though it were an ordinary Warhol poster and not an original print. My jaw dropped. It was the perfect disguise. Grace sold for less than she was worth. Those are the three Ds that make people sell urgently, my gallerist friend said: Death, Divorce, Debt. I could see a thin line stretch and tear audibly between Grace Kelly and Maiberg as we came out of the gallery. His car boot was lighter, his bank account fuller, but it seemed that a tiny hollow had been added to his heart. How many hollows like that are added in the course of a whole life? How many do you need before there is more hollow than content?

We drove back to Maine and I devoted the following days to exploring the island next to Maiberg's house; he was the perfect host. He let me disappear to a floor of my own, and when I came back from my wanderings he served good food on a legendary table on which you could feel the memories, like the rings left by glasses. I drove without purpose on Deer Isle, which connects to the mainland by a narrow bridge and then by a thin strip of road that looks as though it is one wave away from melting into the water. One of those bridges where it is not clear whether your heart skips a beat because it is beautiful or because death is around the corner. On the islands I found beaches, lakes and lobster-roll kiosks.

I had imagined how hard and how exhausting it is to be a parent, and how satisfying, but I had no idea how much I would miss being alone. Crossing a lake, lying down on a rock in the middle of it and coming back slowly in long strokes. And when you have had enough of being alone, striking up a conversation with a person on a path, discovering a hidden lobster beach with him, swimming naked because you can, and developing the kind of soul-conversation that can only be woven between two naked strangers beside an inlet tucked inside a cliff. We met again by chance the next day; he was staying at an ecological guesthouse, a self-sufficient sustainability compound not connected to electricity. His partner was staying at a well-known craft art centre a few minutes' drive away. It is a spectacular and astonishingly symmetrical building, and I went to see it after visiting hours. My new friend said he was an artist, that he was going to lunch at the house of the director of Hedwig and the Angry Inch, on the island. I think we each only half believed the other's stories. What are the chances of meeting, on a remote beach, a journalist who has just transported a Warhol print? What are the chances that I would meet there an artist who had shown at the Whitney and at the Smithsonian, the national gallery in the capital?

I said goodbye to Maiberg and set out for my original destination, Cape Cod. I arrived before my friend did, and so I spent a whole evening with a group of childhood friends who have spent every summer at the lake since they were babies, with no direct connection to them. Even so, I felt at home. All of them, like me, were the children of Soviet Jewish immigrants. Some had spent time in Israel. Later, around the fire, I told them the story of how I found myself driving to New York with Grace Kelly in the boot. They drank the story down thirstily along with cold beers, and at the end of it they came up to me to make sure it was all true and to see the photograph, the only proof of the journey. Grace walking around New York exposed, as though she were not worth half a Montreal house.
