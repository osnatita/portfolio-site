Blame Canada

תאשימו את קנדה

Osnat Ita Skoblinski · Montreal, March 2016

The rise of a progressive and handsome prime minister has the world drooling over Canada, and the victories Donald Trump is collecting in the primaries have sent the search term “how to emigrate to Canada” leaping in the United States. Is Canada really the new wet dream, and is Trudeau really a leader of a new kind?

Imagine for a moment a parallel world in which Yair Lapid is elected prime minister of Israel, and instead of waffling about Dulcinea and embarrassing himself on Facebook, he says all the right things, performs all the right symbolic acts, and the world is in ecstasy over Israel's handsome, liberal prime minister. That is more or less the Israeli equivalent of what is happening now in Canada with the election of the Liberal party leader, Justin Trudeau, as prime minister in October 2015. The victories Donald Trump is collecting in the primaries have sent the search term “how to emigrate to Canada” leaping in the United States, and “because it's 2015” — Trudeau's knockout answer to the question of why half his cabinet is made up of women — has become a calling card that echoed around the world. Not a bad achievement for a country considered America's little sister. Not a bad achievement for a politician who has only just been elected.

Trudeau, son of the legendary prime minister Pierre Trudeau — the one who led Canada to become the social-democratic and liberal country it is today — is a wizard of communication. He knows how to work with today's media and he knows which symbolic acts will please anyone who considers themselves progressive. The internet is flooded with videos of him dancing bhangra, with stripped-down photographs of him as a muscular boxer, and with videos in which he greets Syrian refugees newly arrived in Canada. The hype led the style section of The New York Times to publish a piece wondering whether Canada had suddenly become cool with Trudeau's rise to power. The same piece named Canadians who are succeeding in the world, like the rapper Drake and the actor Michael Gosling.

Has Canada really become cool? Should we believe the hype? And is Trudeau really a prime minister of a new kind — young, handsome, feminist, left-wing, colourful? To answer that you have to ask what makes a leader a genuine driver of change, and what makes a place cool.

“It's refreshing, it's a generational change and you can feel it, but for the time being it's one big show,” says a senior political adviser on the Canadian left. “It's a huge contrast with the outdated style of the previous prime minister. Stephen Harper was the old uncle, frustrated at losing his dominance and the dominance of his reference group in the world; he refused to understand that the world was changing and that things weren't going to work the same way. Harper was a real conservative, he wanted to go back to other times. Unlike him, Trudeau is a son of the period — but you have to remember he is still a white, privileged man. We got a forty-two-year-old instead of the old uncle, but so far he is mostly busy uploading selfies of himself doing cool things rather than dealing with real content. It's nice that he gets photographed with Syrian refugees, but most of them arrive today through private sponsors. That is, ordinary people pay tens of thousands of dollars and take responsibility for the refugees who arrive. While he takes the selfie with the refugee, somebody else is paying the bill.”

Show or not, it is hard to stay indifferent to Trudeau's record so far. As noted, half his cabinet ministers are women; the justice minister is a leader within the Indigenous community; the defence minister is a Sikh immigrant from India. This month he met four ordinary citizens who asked him questions on television. Unlike conservative leaders, who rarely give interviews, he is accessible to the media and creates a strong impression of accessibility to his voters.

It is hard to institute real change in half a year in office. Trudeau's actual work will be visible when the government's annual federal budget is published on 22 March 2016. The annual budget is the backbone of Canadian politics, and in it you can see where the money will go: will the government decide to tackle global warming, public housing, employment, higher taxation of the rich, or investment in public transport? The budget will show whether he really follows his father. When Pierre Trudeau was elected in 1968 he brought a genuine message: he allocated substantial resources to culture, nationalised the oil industry, and started Trudeaumania. It was a period of great excitement in a people thirsty for change, which had abandoned religion and adored the charming, eccentric intellectual who over ten years in office would turn Canada into what it is today. Trudeau the father cultivated the idea of Canada as a mosaic of cultures, an appreciation of multiculturalism that did not require immigrants to abandon their past in order to become Canadian — a fairly revolutionary idea at a time when countries like the United States or Israel encouraged a melting pot that demanded a new national identity at the expense of immigrants' heritage and identity.

So what is actually happening in Canada?

“The country that gave the world ice hockey, the snowplough and Labatt beer,” is how the New York Times correspondent described Canada. The condescending description joins the pile of American clichés about the neighbours to the north: too nice, lumberjacks, maple-sniffers, moose-riders. But the truth is that Canada, objectively, is a far better place to live. True, it has no cultural and economic blows like New York or San Francisco, and yes, it has a hard, long winter — but it has health insurance, education, and respectable budgets for art and culture. Its rate of higher education is among the highest in the world, and studies show Canadians are among the happiest peoples in the developed world.

Canada has always produced artists, and comedians especially. The musician Drake, the actor Ryan Gosling and the director Xavier Dolan are some of the names mentioned today in the context of Canada's rising stock. All of them succeeded long before Trudeau's rise, and a glance backwards throws up more: Leonard Cohen, Donald Sutherland, Neil Young, Joni Mitchell, Mike Myers, Jim Carrey, Nora Ephron, Alanis Morissette. “Canada was always the underdog, and so comedians always came out of here and it always had representatives in Hollywood,” explains Sandy Dobia, a Canadian journalist. “Canadians have the unique perspective of somebody who is allowed to be inside but not entirely, who watches a little from the side and criticises what's happening. You can be a lot funnier when you're looking at the forest. The Americans are looking at the roots of the trees.”

If things are so good in Canada, why did many of the artists listed above leave it? Part of the reason is the very thing that makes Canada genuinely cool — it doesn't really care. I watched with my own eyes as Michael Cera sat in a café in Montreal and nobody gave him a second look. Nobody comes to Canada to succeed; people come to Canada to live well. Canada is a place that aspires to mediocrity, in a good way. The American myth of the land of the free was trampled long ago by infinite ambition and capitalist competitiveness. How free can you really be when you are permanently occupied chasing first place and the big house? Canadians say you can be yourself in Canada more than anywhere else in the world, and there is something in that. The only stars who get hysteria in the street are hockey stars — and that is in fact its great weak point. Here Canada loses its coolness entirely: in the Canadian ice rink, losing is not an option. Genuinely cool people are embarrassed to be at the top of the pyramid; they prefer to look at the successful from the side, with the contempt reserved for those who try too hard.

Little sister complex

When Canadian celebrities succeed abroad, Canadians fill with pride. They still need the world's confirmation that they are all right, that they are talented. Canadians suffer from the provincial complex we know so well in Israel: whoever succeeded abroad has really succeeded. The piece published in the New York Times led to dozens of articles reporting to Canadians that here, finally, the Americans recognise their coolness. The responses to the piece — even those that mocked it — proved that despite everything said above, Canada is still the little sister pretending she doesn't need the big brother's approval while deep down desperately needing one. In the end, in many ways, Canada does not maintain a culture separate from America, and the cultural differences within it are not necessarily greater than the cultural differences between different states in the US. With one exception: Quebec.

The cities of Canada

It is hard to speak about Canada in generalisations; this is a country of thirty-five million people spread across a landmass second in size only to Russia. It has inter-regional tensions and no shortage of internal Canadian stereotypes. Vancouver, in British Columbia, carries the stereotypes of the West Coast: a horrifyingly expensive city whose residents love yoga, New Age and activity in the spectacular nature that surrounds them. Toronto, the largest city in Canada, whose residents like to think of it as the Canadian New York, holds one of the largest concentrations of immigrants in the world. Half its residents were born outside Canada and about a hundred and forty languages and dialects are spoken there. On a long walk down Bloor Street you can find authentic restaurants from every corner of the world. Toronto is also Canada's business centre and many people come to it to find work. “It's a city of workers,” says Joe Hamra, an advertising man of forty-five who has spent his whole life in the city. “People come here to get a job, and most people work hard in order to afford to live here. It's a big business centre, and after work you can go down to the ground floor and be sure there'll be a bar or a good restaurant there. Rent is very high and it's got worse in recent years. In Toronto everyone works and barely survives the month.”

The prices in Toronto that force its residents to work so hard are dwarfed by the rise in property prices in Canada's third-largest metropolis, Vancouver. Property prices there are the highest in Canada, reaching an average of more than a million dollars for a house. So why, even so? Vancouver is regularly ranked as one of the cities with the highest quality of life in the world, and its residents will swear there is no more beautiful city — or, more precisely, that no city is set in such spectacular surroundings. “What's amazing there is what surrounds it,” says Erica Richmond, a landscape architect who studied and spent most of her twenties in the city. “The only thing you want to do there is be outdoors; that's why people live there, not because there's great culture or a nightlife scene, although that is improving too. You can take a bus to one of the ski hills or to an epic twelve-hour hike. In the city itself the beaches are wild and you can get to them by bike. Everyone cycles everywhere, and the hills keep everybody fit. If you're not fit you'll feel like shit about yourself. A friend of mine called it a fascist body culture. In Vancouver it's completely normal to go out to dinner in Lululemon trousers. People wear super-hi-tech trekking clothes, with velcro, everything engineered and waterproof against the rain that falls all the time.” The cost of living in Vancouver pushed Erica, like many of her friends, to move to Toronto, where more opportunities and higher salaries were waiting.

The best-kept secret in North America — Montreal

You don't have to pay for Canada's high quality of life with excessive work or impossible rent. I spent most of the past year in Montreal, one of the best-kept secrets in North America. Montreal is the biggest city (close to four million people in the metropolitan area) in the francophone province of Quebec, and the second-biggest in Canada. Its rents recall Berlin's prices five years ago, its culture budgets are rich, and it is one of the central cultural stations in North America, with one festival chasing another and appearances by the most talked-about bands in the world.

Cheap rent allows more than a high standard of living; it allows creative people to take risks and do what actually interests them. The absence of economic pressure, and a culture that does not glorify people like Donald Trump, makes room to operate outside the norms, from motives that are not necessarily economic. Today's rent is a fortune compared with the period that began in the mid-eighties and ran until the early 2000s. Quebec's aspirations to independence drove away many business owners; industrialists, bankers and English-speaking proprietors left, among them many Jews, and Montreal was on the edge of economic bankruptcy. Property prices collapsed, shops closed, and the vacuum that formed drew many young people who moved in and created a strong music scene. Bands like Wolf Parade, Godspeed You! Black Emperor, Tiga and later Arcade Fire, Kid Koala and Amon Tobin worked or were formed in Montreal in that period. They lived in student neighbourhoods like the Plateau and Mile End, which today, twenty years later, have become the stronghold of the bourgeoisie.

Jeff Waye, who set up the North American branch of the label Ninja Tune in Montreal, remembers that time: “In the nineties I worked at Cargo Records, which was the hub for every alternative music distribution station in Canada. If you wanted to work in the music industry, that was the place to be. In the early nineties Tiga and his friends started the rave scene in industrial spaces in the city; it connected to the liberal gay scene that was always here. In the seventies Montreal was a disco hit and an obligatory stop alongside New York and Chicago. All of that made Montreal a party city. With businesses fleeing and the city emptying out, people paid three hundred dollars a month for enormous spaces they could live in and also set up rehearsal, performance and recording rooms in. Spin magazine published a piece declaring that Montreal was becoming the next Seattle and that the music scene was happening there. Things still happen here; it's still a very cheap place to live by big-city standards.”

Artists who lived and live in Montreal benefit not only from the cost of living but from the substantial money allocated to culture and entrepreneurship. VICE, for instance, was founded by three friends who studied in Montreal and received a government grant to start the magazine at the end of the nineties. That money gave the first push to the pioneers who shaped the new magazine language of the internet — the same language the New York Times used to wonder whether Canada had become “hip.” The global phenomenon Cirque du Soleil also began in Montreal, from a government grant to street performers. Quebec has the largest culture budget in all of Canada, and world-class cultural institutions operate in Montreal. The art created under that governmental umbrella is not always wonderful, but it allows artists to create and to live in relative comfort.

Multiculturalism and equality are among Canada's great sources of pride today. Canada was one of the first countries to legalise same-sex marriage, and not long ago young Trudeau moved the world with a speech about a school where the children don't learn about other cultures but actually practise authentic openness, celebrating together the festivals of the immigrant families' children. Montreal does not have the cultural diversity of cities like New York or neighbouring Toronto (about six hours' drive to each). The French required here in order to find work deters many immigrants, but the city has a strong intercultural tension between francophones and anglophones. In the improvised bar courtyards that sprout all over the city with the arrival of spring you can hear English and French mixing together. Most young people speak both languages and produce amusing hybrids like très cool or c'est nice. This is not ideal coexistence so much as a status quo. Quebec did not separate from Canada, but many francophones, mainly of the previous generation, still feel a strong Québécois nationalism and are angry that for years, as they see it, their culture was erased by English speakers. A minority will even display a cold or aggressive attitude to anyone who tries to speak to them in English. All the myths about Canadians being too nice do not always hold in this case.

Montreal is divided into French-speaking and English-speaking neighbourhoods, and each community has its own cinematheques, libraries and festivals; the separation does not end there. The Jewish community, one of the largest and oldest in the country, is isolated, entrenched in its conservative neighbourhoods. Immigrants from French-speaking countries like Lebanon and Haiti have their own neighbourhoods. Yes, Montreal is more open and more equal than the big cities in Israel. It is pleasant for women to walk down the street here or go out to bars without too much harassment — but it also has nationalism, xenophobia, Islamophobia. Like everywhere else.

The cultural closedness may prevent the development of new cultures, but it creates a very distinct subculture. Quebec has its own television and radio stations, its own media and its own artists. It has local folk roots and stars who could only have been produced here. It is no accident that a phenomenon like Céline Dion was born in a place where French culture meets American kitsch. In Montreal, a cheap and popular shooting location for the film industry, Hollywood stars can walk around quietly — not only because of the famous Canadian politeness, but because there are francophones who have no idea who they are. There is something admirable about Montreal, which is a province inside a province, a city adjacent to far larger cities, in a country pressed against the most powerful nation in the world, and which nevertheless insists on preserving its own particular culture.

All these unique conditions — the French, the cultural bubbles, the rent, the gay scene, the festivals, the halo of coolness set against economic marginality — make Montreal a very singular place. Montreal is not necessarily perceived as Canadian but as an exotic entity in its own right, a strange francophone North American hybrid creature. A place it is not obvious how exactly it manages to exist, but that you have to visit.

The great challenge for anyone who wants to live here is the winter, and not only because the temperature sometimes drops to minus thirty and the city wears white for almost four consecutive months. The weather also affects people's temperament — this is a city of northern culture. Want to stress a local? Stand close behind them in the bus queue. People here will leave you alone and keep at least a metre and a half of distance in a queue for public transport. Try to start a conversation and they will answer politely and go back to their business. The only way to cope with the cold is to adopt the accessible winter activities. In Montreal you can feel your taxes coming back to you. There are parks on every corner, and in almost every one of them the city and the residents maintain hockey rinks and skating areas. Every day they plough cross-country ski trails in the central park, Mont Royal. Half an hour to an hour's drive and you can forget you are in a city and spend the day skiing down the trails of spectacular parks. Want to get out of the city? Within two hours you can reach one of the main ski centres on the east coast. Even people who hate winter stay for the other half of the year — the enormous relief that arrives with the first rays of spring sunshine creates one big party city, fuelled by millions of people who feel as though they have just now experienced sunlight for the first time.

Montreal is a wonderful city for losers. If you are artists looking for budgetary support, cheap rent, access to nature and culture; if you speak French or are prepared to work hard to learn it; if winter doesn't frighten you and snowboarding sounds like something you would enjoy — Montreal is the city for you. If you want to succeed, to conquer the world, to feel you are swimming in the sweaty navel of everything happening right now, look for somewhere else, and come here on holiday.

Sidebar: Immigration

Does North American quality of life plus Scandinavian socialism do it for you? Does the appreciation of culture and the generous budgets tempt you? The gates of the land of maple are not as open as they once were, but there is still hope. Despite Canada's reputation as a country open to immigration, the conditions of entry became more rigid over a decade of conservative government. According to Emily Altschuler, a certified adviser in Canadian immigration law, the borders are going to open further soon under the Liberal government. “The Conservatives narrowed the conditions of entry. As of today it looks like we're returning to the classic immigration system that was shaped by Liberal government in recent decades. You can already see it on the ground with the acceptance of refugees. You really feel the difference in family reunification cases: the government doubled the quota in that category. Processing time for all immigration files has also shortened, especially in humanitarian cases. In the refugee field there is a good deal of propaganda: today there are fewer than three hundred refugees from Syria who arrived without sponsorship. In the past Canada took in refugees and financed their settlement process; today the state requires somebody to sponsor them — a community or a family. That is something that changed in the last decade under the Conservative government, and I don't believe it will change.”

What are the options for Israelis who want to emigrate to Canada?

“In the past most Israeli Canadians immigrated here thanks to the points system, which lets you accumulate points according to level of education, in-demand professions, age and other criteria. Today those conditions of entry don't exist at the federal level, only in Quebec. There, in order to accumulate enough points, you need a level of command of French or a specialisation in an in-demand profession like programming or genetics. That programme also has a limited quota — in June it will open to only five thousand candidates from around the world.

“From 2015 the immigration process to Canada became harder. People come first as temporary residents: as students, or on a work visa. They have to show excellence in their studies or in their field of work over several years, and only then do they submit immigration papers.”
