Zero in Decency: Nissan Shor Doesn't Really Regret What He Wrote About Me

אפס בהגינות: ניסן שור לא באמת מתחרט על מה שכתב עליי

Osnat Ita Skoblinski · Haaretz · 30 November 2017

When Nissan Shor wrote in the paper that I was vulgar, that I should be sued, that he doubted whether the man who grabbed my backside three times after I asked him to stop had any sexual intentions towards me, and the rest of the insults, everybody advised me not to respond. You ran a successful campaign, they told me. You went public on Facebook against a man who sexually assaulted you and other women, you created a conversation about it, and the assailant apologised publicly. Nissan Shor isn't even worth a response, they said. In those days the media treated my case as a sensation, and I had to listen to all sorts of men in television studios explaining why my course of action was not legitimate. I refused to appear on the programmes that invited me. When I wrote Nissan Shor a reply in my own voice and sent it to the opinion desks, they didn't want to publish it, so I let it go. Through the years when I made my living writing freelance in the media, I was afraid to speak out against the journalists who had spoken out against me, who are still in positions of power, including at this paper.

I have stopped being afraid. Last week I wrote a post on Facebook and aired all the anger and humiliation still left over towards everyone who wrote disgusting things about me in the press, and there were plenty of them. I had a bellyful about the women who cursed me, and about everyone who turned me into a controversial figure. I was probably the first woman in Israel to use the platform of social media to expose someone who had assaulted her. The question that kept coming, “Why didn't you go to the police?!” seemed legitimate then. Today it is perfectly clear what happens if you go to the police and say a man in a bar touched you, and clear what happens if you dare complain about something much worse, like rape. Today, going public against assailants is not exceptional. It has become a wave that cannot be ignored.

The enormous resentment I felt towards the women complaining today, who receive support I never got, was released after I wrote that post. The dozens of women who wrote to me publicly and privately about how much my case gave them, and still gives them, strength and inspiration, reminded me that although I lost a great deal, it was worth it. I felt at the time that I had been left alone in the battle. I left the city I loved, and in the end I left the country too. Today Nissan Shor is aggrieved that I have blocked him on Facebook.

When you go public against someone who harassed or assaulted you, you reawaken your own trauma. You are talking about a moment in which every right over your own body was taken from you. I remember what I was wearing that night nearly nine years ago. Every word that disagrees with you feels like a punch in the stomach; every attack feels like a lash on bare skin. When someone writes such appalling things about you in a newspaper, and adds that you ought to be sued, he effectively becomes your assailant number two. Nissan Shor; Ran Oren, who apologised and then smeared me in the press anyway; and the rest of the enlightened men who defended their friend, are all part of my trauma. Seeing their names makes me recoil, all the more so seeing their faces around town. We are not standing on opposite sides of a barricade throwing stones at each other, as he writes. I am a woman who stood exposed while you threw stones at her from behind the wall of power you had. Everyone who came out against me then has been blocked by me for years.

Shor's column is not really a beating of the breast. If he had wanted to write me an apology (and I never said I wasn't interested in one, as he claims), he could have sent it to me in any number of ways. You know what? He could simply have published an apology in the paper, instead of writing a column whose entire purpose is to clear his name. A person who truly repents does not mention that there are other men who behave as he does, or report that even feminists consort with rapists, or drag everyone down to his own standards in order to demonstrate that it isn't him, it's society, and that it is the human soul that is sick. He certainly does not tell her friends that the inaccuracy in what she wrote is grounds for a libel suit. (Nissan appears to have a fondness for the combination of my name and the words “libel suit.”)

You don't have to be a feminist not to attack a woman who came out against the man who assaulted her. You have to be a decent person. To beat your breast you don't have to wait until people start sending you abuse because the woman you attacked has finally expressed her anger; it is enough to be a decent person. To apologise for real, rather than write a text that mostly serves you, it is enough to be a decent person. Maybe Shor's consciousness has changed along with “the spirit of the times,” but there are things that are hard to develop, like basic decency.

A good friend who works in therapy told me that we often feel anxiety when we store up anger at people and don't express it. She encouraged me to write to all the people I felt had wronged me. She was right. Today, after writing about all the people who attacked me, I am no longer angry. The world becomes a safer and more equal place for women with every year that passes, but there is a great deal of work ahead of us. I don't know how the #MeToo movement will end, but I know that it is part of what will make this world better. People who attack complainants are negligible, and “the spirit of the times” will deal with them. Keep talking about it. Bring every skeleton with the overly long arms out into the light. It will not be easy, but in the end it will be a repair. I have to be convinced that the suffering we go through when we open our mouths is not for nothing.

I don't want to conclude my story. I want it to be a continuation of every woman who cleared stones from the terrifying mound standing in our way, and a beginning for all the women who will carry on with this Sisyphean work. Yes, sometimes a few stones will roll back down towards us, but we are strong enough to withstand them. Here is some advice for anyone about to complain, and for those who want to support her. This is not a manual, only the lessons of my own experience. I invite you to add your own thoughts in the comments.

About to complain publicly about harassment or sexual assault?

Know that it will not be easy. As above, the emotional exposure will be hard, and may provoke a counterattack from the assailant, his friends and his lawyers. Take a deep breath. Remember the bigger picture: you are making this world safer for all women. You are dealing with your trauma, even if it sometimes seems that your surroundings are only amplifying it.

A public complaint is a very personal exposure. Before you tell the world, gather a few people you trust around you. Friends and allies who will support you emotionally from beginning to end. In cases where you know it will reach the media, consult friends and colleagues in the field. The fight in the public arena is not simple, emotionally or strategically.

Prepare yourself for the fact that women who were assaulted by the same man will not always join your public fight. Not everyone is in the right place to start a battle. The fact that you decided to go public now does not mean it is the right time for them. Don't be angry at them. They are still your allies.

Want to support a complainant?

Support her publicly. Express support in a post on social media, in a comment on what she writes, or in an opinion piece.

Say publicly, or to her privately, if you have heard about similar cases or if the assailant hurt you too. Even if you don't join her publicly, she will feel less alone.

Write her a message or a letter of support. She won't always have the strength to answer you, but I don't have words enough to describe how much it holds a person up.

Share her story publicly and with your friends. You may hear that way about another woman it happened to.

Come out against anyone who dares come out against her. There will always be more attackers than complainants, and they need you behind them at the front line.

After consulting her, set up a support group for her fight, or join one if it already exists.

Advise her from your own experience, if you feel you have something to contribute.

Share your own story with her.

Connect her with other women who have been through something similar.

When you see her, hug her (with consent) and tell her how important you think what she is doing is.
